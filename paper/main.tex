\begin{abstract}
Transformers consuming several time-aligned scalar signals (e.g.\
multivariate sensor streams or multi-modal feature channels) typically
embed each channel independently and \emph{sum} the embeddings before
self-attention. We study the most common form of this recipe---a
\emph{shared} scalar projection plus per-channel additive embedding---and
show that on a benchmark where channel identity is informative it has a
sharp ceiling that extra model capacity does not move: validation
negative log-likelihood (NLL) stays within $0.02$ nats of $3.25$ across
a $15\times$ parameter sweep. Two simple fixes recover the full gap and
reach $\sim$2.42~NLL. The first, \textbf{concat}, reserves a
non-overlapping block of embedding dimensions per channel and concatenates
them. The second, \textbf{sum-ortho}, keeps summation but adds a soft
orthogonality regulariser $\lambda \sum_{i\ne j}(W_i{\cdot}W_j)^2$ on the
per-channel learned projections. Both cut the NLL gap to the uniform
baseline by $\sim$84\% and more than double top-1 bin accuracy over naive
sum; they match each other to within seed std. We also evaluate a
frequency-division-multiplexing-inspired variant (\textbf{fdm}), which
amplitude-modulates a channel-specific sinusoidal carrier inside each
block: FDM converges faster but saturates at a worse ceiling
(NLL $2.85$), with a grid search over carrier bands confirming the gap is
structural, not a hyperparameter choice. Channel-as-token and
channel-independent architectural baselines both underperform at matched
parameter budget. A linear-probe diagnostic shows that block partitioning
and soft orthogonality both yield input embeddings from which each raw
channel is recoverable to $R^2 \approx 1.0$, preserved through attention
to $R^2 \approx 0.93$ at the final layer; naive summation collapses to
$R^2 \approx 0.24$ at every layer. We conclude that channel identity is
what matters, and two cheap mechanisms---hard partitioning or soft
orthogonality---are both adequate ways to supply it.
Code: \url{https://github.com/OssiLehtinen/FDM-encoding}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A canonical use of transformers outside language is fitting a sequence
model to several time-aligned numerical signals: multi-sensor telemetry,
financial factor stacks, biomedical waveforms, multi-modal embeddings
along time. The standard recipe inherited from vision and language
\citep{vaswani2017attention} maps each scalar input to a vector and
\emph{adds} those vectors together---the same trick used to inject
positional information. We will refer to this as the \emph{sum}
encoding.

When the input is multiple \emph{simultaneous} channels rather than a
single token-plus-position pair, summation has a potential structural
problem: given $\mathbf{h} = \sum_k W_k x_k$, the network cannot recover
individual $x_k$ from $\mathbf{h}$ unless the $W_k$ occupy orthogonal
subspaces. Nothing in the standard loss enforces this. Our empirical
finding is specific: the default shared-projection summation used in many
practitioner codebases does not, under training, allocate orthogonal
subspaces on its own, and a $15\times$ parameter sweep does not fix it.
Forcing orthogonality---either by hard block partitioning, or by a soft
penalty on learned per-channel projections---recovers the full gap.

The paper also addresses a communications-inspired variant, FDM, in
which each channel's scalar value amplitude-modulates a sinusoidal
carrier at a channel-specific frequency \citep{su2021roformer}. Despite
the name, FDM in a non-overlapping-block embedding does not prevent
``cross-talk''---the blocks do not overlap, so there is no cross-talk to
prevent. What the sinusoidal carrier does provide is an intra-block,
RoPE-style positional prior. We find that this is a
\emph{convergence-speed advantage}: FDM reaches a given NLL sooner than
block partitioning with a learned projection, but is overtaken at
convergence and falls further behind as the per-channel block grows
wider.

We study three encoder mechanisms on the same backbone:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Per-channel projection, summed} (\textsc{sum-perch}):
      keep summation but give each channel its own learned $W_k$
      instead of a shared $W$.
\item \textbf{Per-channel projection, summed, plus soft orthogonality}
      (\textsc{sum-ortho}): as above, with an auxiliary loss
      $\lambda \sum_{i \ne j}(W_i{\cdot}W_j)^2$ that pulls the $W_k$
      toward orthogonal directions.
\item \textbf{Block partitioning} (\textsc{concat}): reserve a
      $d_{\text{model}}/C$-dim embedding block per channel and
      concatenate; channel identity is a hard architectural property.
\end{itemize}
We also evaluate \textsc{fdm}: within each \textsc{concat} block, the
scalar value amplitude-modulates a sinusoidal carrier at a
channel-specific frequency, RoPE-style \citep{su2021roformer}. Finally,
we include two architectural baselines from the time-series forecasting
literature: \textsc{ci} (channel-independent, PatchTST-spirit
\citep{nie2023patchtst}) and \textsc{cat} (channel-as-token,
iTransformer-spirit \citep{liu2024itransformer}).

\paragraph{Key findings.}
(i)~\textsc{sum-perch}, \textsc{sum-ortho}, and \textsc{concat} reach
indistinguishable downstream metrics---\emph{per-channel learned
projections are the mechanism}; whether they are summed, summed with an
orthogonality penalty, or hard-partitioned into disjoint blocks is
incidental.
(ii)~An explicit gram-matrix measurement shows that \textsc{sum-perch}'s
learned $W_k$ already converge to near-orthogonal subspaces without
regularisation; \textsc{sum-ortho}'s penalty tightens this further but
does not change downstream performance.
(iii)~\textsc{fdm}'s sinusoidal carrier is a faster-convergence bias
but a worse ceiling, because amplitude-modulating a fixed template has
fewer degrees of freedom than a learned projection once the block is
wide enough to exploit; the gap does not close under an 11-point
carrier-band grid search or a learnable-$\omega_k$ variant.
(iv)~The architectural baselines \textsc{ci} and \textsc{cat} do not
close the gap at matched parameter budget.
(v)~A linear-probe diagnostic, a test-time channel-mask ablation, a
model-width scaling study, and a real-dataset run on ETTh1 all
consistently rank encoders the same way as the downstream metric.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item A controlled benchmark with lagged cross-channel interactions
      designed to expose whether an encoder preserves channel identity,
      plus a real-dataset complement on ETTh1.
\item A seven-way encoder comparison (\textsc{sum}, \textsc{sum-perch},
      \textsc{sum-ortho}, \textsc{concat}, \textsc{fdm}, \textsc{ci},
      \textsc{cat}) at matched sequence length and total parameter
      budget, over 5 seeds with 100-epoch cosine-scheduled training.
\item A decomposition of the ``soft orthogonality'' fix into its two
      ingredients---replacing shared $W$ with per-channel $W_k$
      (\textsc{sum-perch}) and adding an orthogonality penalty
      (\textsc{sum-ortho})---showing that the per-channel projections
      carry essentially all of the improvement.
\item A direct gram-matrix measurement confirming that per-channel
      learned projections converge to near-orthogonal subspaces
      spontaneously under the task loss alone.
\item A reframing of FDM-style carrier encodings as a
      convergence-speed inductive bias rather than a better ceiling,
      supported by a width sweep ($d_{\text{model}} \in \{64, 128, 256\}$),
      an 11-point carrier-band grid search, and a learnable-$\omega_k$
      control.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Problem setup}
We are given a sequence $\{x_t\}_{t=1}^T$ where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^C$ is
a vector of $C$ scalar channels at time $t$. We want a transformer to
model $p(y_t \mid x_{\le t-1})$ where $y_t$ is a downstream target,
binned into $K$ classes for a categorical generative head. The question
we address is purely about how $x_t$ enters the residual stream.

\subsection{Encoders}

\paragraph{\textsc{sum}.} Baseline:
\[
  \mathbf{h}(t) = \sum_k (W v_k(t) + e_k) + \mathbf{p}(t),
\]
with shared scalar projection $W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}\times 1}$,
learned channel embedding $e_k$, and sinusoidal positions $\mathbf{p}(t)$.

\paragraph{\textsc{sum-perch}.} Like \textsc{sum} but with a per-channel
learned projection $W_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$ and per-channel
bias $b_k$:
\[
  \mathbf{h}(t) = \sum_k (W_k\,v_k(t) + b_k) + \mathbf{p}(t).
\]
No orthogonality penalty. Channel identity is something the optimiser
must discover through the task loss alone.

\paragraph{\textsc{sum-ortho}.} Identical encoder to \textsc{sum-perch}
with an auxiliary orthogonality loss
\[
  L_{\text{ortho}} = \lambda \sum_{i \ne j} (W_i \cdot W_j)^2 / 2,
\]
added to the task NLL; we use $\lambda {=} 10^{-2}$. The difference
between \textsc{sum-perch} and \textsc{sum-ortho} is exactly the
contribution of the regulariser.

\paragraph{\textsc{concat}.} Reserve
$d_{\text{block}} {=} d_{\text{model}}/C$ dims per channel and
concatenate:
\[
  \mathbf{e}_k(t) = W_k\,v_k(t) + b_k, \quad
  \mathbf{h}(t) = [\mathbf{e}_0(t) \Vert \cdots \Vert \mathbf{e}_{C-1}(t)]
                  + \mathbf{p}(t).
\]
Channel identity is a hard structural property at the input layer.

\paragraph{\textsc{fdm}.} Like \textsc{concat}, but the per-block
projection is replaced by a sinusoidal carrier amplitude-modulated by
the scalar value. For channel $k$ at time $t$, with base $b{=}10000$ and
carrier $\omega_k$ log-spaced across $[0.5, 8.0]$:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{e}_k(t)_{2i}   = v_k(t)\,\sin\!\left(\omega_k\, t\, b^{-2i/d_{\text{block}}}\right), \quad
  \mathbf{e}_k(t)_{2i+1} = v_k(t)\,\cos\!\left(\omega_k\, t\, b^{-2i/d_{\text{block}}}\right),
  \label{eq:fdm}
\end{equation}
for $i = 0,\dots,d_{\text{block}}/2 - 1$. The encoder is parameter-free
(learnable $\omega_k$ is evaluated as a separate variant, \textsc{fdm-learn}).
Attention dot products between two positions in the same channel block
become $v_k(t_1) v_k(t_2)\,\langle\phi(t_1),\phi(t_2)\rangle$, exposing
lag-dependent intra-channel correlations directly to the softmax---a
RoPE-style intra-channel positional prior.

\paragraph{\textsc{ci} (channel-independent).} PatchTST-spirit
\citep{nie2023patchtst}: one causal transformer is run with shared
weights over each channel's univariate sequence; final hidden states at
each time are concatenated into the head. Each channel gets full
$d_{\text{model}}$ capacity; compute scales as $\mathcal{O}(C B T^2)$.

\paragraph{\textsc{cat} (channel-as-token).} iTransformer-spirit
\citep{liu2024itransformer}: each $(t, k)$ pair becomes its own token,
sequence length is $C{\cdot}T$, and a standard transformer runs on the
flattened stream. The mask is causal across time and
\emph{bi-directional within a time step} (tokens at the same $t$ see
each other freely; tokens at $t'>t$ are masked out), so we impose no
arbitrary intra-time channel ordering. Prediction at time $t$ is
obtained by mean-pooling the $C$ per-channel tokens at $t$. Attention
cost is $\mathcal{O}((CT)^2)$; we skip this baseline at $C{=}16$.

\subsection{Dataset}
We synthesise $N{=}512$ time series of length $T{=}160$ with $C$ channels
each. Each channel is an independent stochastic process: a sum of three
sinusoids with random frequencies in $[0.005, 0.08]$ cycles/sample
(angular $\approx [0.03, 0.50]\,\text{rad/sample}$), random phases, and
first-order autoregressive [AR(1)] Gaussian noise; signals are then
standardised. The outcome is a fixed non-linear function of the
\emph{first four} channels with two different lags,
\[
y_t = \tanh\!\big(s_0(t{-}3)\,s_1(t)\big)
      + 0.6\sin\!\big(1.3\,s_2(t{-}7)\big)
      + 0.4\,\mathbb{1}[s_3(t)>0]\,s_0(t),
\]
binned into $K{=}32$ quantile bins. Any additional channels
($k \ge 4$) are independent distractors. The signal content occupies
$[0.03, 0.5]\,\text{rad/sample}$; FDM carriers live in
$[0.5, 8.0]\,\text{rad/sample}$, so the \textsc{fdm} gain is not a
trivial resonance with the data.

\subsection{Architecture and training}
A small causal transformer encoder with $d_{\text{model}}{=}64$, 4
heads, 3 layers, feed-forward network (FFN) width 256, dropout $0.1$,
pre-LayerNorm. The head is a linear map to $K{=}32$ logits. Training:
AdamW, lr $3{\times}10^{-4}$ with cosine decay to $1\%$ of peak, weight
decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 32, gradient clip 1.0, 100 epochs. Each
result is the mean over 5 random seeds (3 for the carrier grid).
Metrics are reported at the best val NLL achieved during training
(effective early stopping); trajectories are saved so convergence can be
audited.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Main comparison}
\label{sec:results-main}

\input{tables/main.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:main} reports all seven encoders at $C{=}4$,
$d_{\text{model}}{=}64$. Three encoders tie at the top, within one seed
std ($\sim$0.02 NLL): \textsc{sum-perch}, \textsc{sum-ortho}, and
\textsc{concat}. All three more than double \textsc{sum}'s bin accuracy.
Notably, \textsc{sum-perch} matches \textsc{sum-ortho} to four decimal
places of NLL---the orthogonality penalty does not move downstream
performance at all on this task. This reframes the earlier ``soft
orthogonality'' mechanism as a small decorator on top of the real fix,
which is simply giving each channel its own learned projection.
\textsc{fdm} sits $\sim$0.4 nats behind the top tier, clearly beating
\textsc{sum} but not matching what a learned per-block projection does.
The architectural baselines \textsc{ci} and \textsc{cat} underperform
all three top encoders at the same parameter budget, despite giving
each channel full $d_{\text{model}}$ capacity (ci) or $C\cdot T$
sequence length (cat).

Table~\ref{tab:dmodel} and Figure~\ref{fig:dmodel} vary
$d_{\text{model}}$ over $\{64, 128, 256\}$ with

\textsc{sum} is structurally capped: total parameter count grows
$15\times$ across the sweep, and validation NLL changes by less than
$0.02$. No amount of width fixes rank-deficient encoding. \textsc{concat}
scales strongly from $2.429$ at $d{=}64$ down to $2.291$ at $d{=}128$

The gap between \textsc{concat} and \textsc{fdm} widens with width,
consistent with the fixed sinusoidal template becoming a ceiling
constraint once the block has room to learn a richer projection.

\paragraph{Is it the frequency choice?}
We ran two controls at $d_{\text{model}}{=}128$. (i)~A
\textsc{fdm-learn} variant with $\omega_k$ as learnable parameters
(initialised at the log-spaced defaults) changes NLL and accuracy by
less than $10^{-3}$: diagnostics show the $\omega_k$ drift by $<1\%$
under AdamW in 100 epochs because the gradient signal to four scalar
frequencies is dominated by the surrounding Q/K/V/FFN weights.
(ii)~A grid search over 11 fixed carrier bands
$(\omega_{\min}, \omega_{\max})$ spanning $[0.03, 0.5]$ (matching the
signal bandwidth) through $[10, 100]$ yields a best NLL of
$2.809 \pm 0.003$ for the signal-matched band---better than the default
$2.900$ by ${\sim}0.09$ nats, but still $0.52$ nats behind
\textsc{concat}'s $2.291$. \emph{No fixed carrier band closes the gap.}

\label{sec:results-gram}

metrics? We measure, at the end of training, the cosine of every pair
$(W_i, W_j)$ with $i \ne j$ in the encoder's per-channel projection
matrix.

Without any penalty, the learned $W_k$ already end training with mean
off-diagonal cosine $0.054$; the regulariser pushes that down to $0.021$
but does not change the downstream NLL by more than $10^{-4}$. We
interpret this as evidence that on tasks where channel identity is
informative, the downstream loss itself provides enough gradient
pressure to drive per-channel projections toward orthogonal subspaces,
once those projections exist as independent parameters. The penalty
\emph{accelerates} this discovery but is not what makes it happen.

\subsection{Channel identifiability via linear probing}
\label{sec:results-probe}

\input{tables/probe.tex}

We freeze a trained model and fit closed-form ridge probes
$\mathbf{h} \mapsto \hat{x}$ from the hidden state at layer $\ell$ to
the raw input $x$, reporting held-out per-channel $R^2$
(Table~\ref{tab:probe}). All four per-channel-$W_k$ encoders

\subsection{Real-dataset complement: ETTh1}
\label{sec:results-etth1}

\input{tables/etth1.tex}

To check that the synthetic-benchmark ordering is not an artefact of the
task construction, we repeat the comparison on the standard ETTh1
dataset (Electricity Transformer Temperature, hourly sampling, 7

standardise each variate on a train split, bin the target variate
\texttt{OT} (oil temperature) into 32 quantile bins from the train

given all 7 variates over a $T{=}160$ window. The model uses
$d_{\text{model}}{=}56$ (divisible by $C{=}7$), 7 heads, 3 layers,
$d_{\text{ff}}{=}224$; training is otherwise identical (100 epochs,
cosine decay, AdamW, 5 seeds).

Table~\ref{tab:etth1} shows the same qualitative ordering as the
synthetic benchmark. \textsc{sum} fails catastrophically (NLL near the
$\ln 32 \approx 3.466$ ceiling, accuracy $<\!1\%$): the shared-projection
plus additive-channel-embedding recipe cannot extract useful structure
from seven temperature/load variates. The three per-channel-$W_k$
encoders (\textsc{sum-perch}, \textsc{sum-ortho}, \textsc{concat}) are
again tied at the top (NLL $\sim$0.58, accuracy $\sim$0.79), leaving
\textsc{fdm}, \textsc{ci}, and \textsc{cat} behind. The absolute-NLL
gap between \textsc{sum} and the top tier ($\sim$3.1 nats) is even
larger on ETTh1 than on the synthetic task ($\sim$0.8 nats), because
real signals cover more of the categorical head's output space and
\textsc{sum} drops to effectively random.

\label{sec:results-mask}

\input{tables/mask.tex}

At test time we zero one channel at a time and re-evaluate

\section{Discussion}

\paragraph{Three mechanisms converge to the same operating point.}
\textsc{sum-perch}, \textsc{sum-ortho}, and \textsc{concat} are all tied
on downstream NLL, accuracy, $C$-scaling behaviour, probe recoverability,
and mask localisation. They are very different surface recipes: hard
block partitioning with concatenation (\textsc{concat}); per-channel
learned projections summed together (\textsc{sum-perch}); the same
summation with an orthogonality penalty on the projections
(\textsc{sum-ortho}). The common feature is that each channel is
represented by its own learned direction in embedding space. Whether
those directions are enforced to be disjoint by architecture, nudged
toward orthogonal by a regulariser, or simply free to be learned
independently turns out not to matter, because the downstream loss
itself drives them toward near-orthogonal subspaces once they are
free parameters (Section~\ref{sec:results-gram}, Table~\ref{tab:gram}:
mean off-diagonal cosine $\approx 0.05$ for \textsc{sum-perch} with no
penalty).

\paragraph{Practical implication.}
\textsc{sum-perch} is the cheapest retrofit for legacy sum-encoding
pipelines: a one-line change ($W \rightarrow W_k$ indexed by channel),
$\mathcal{O}(C \cdot d_{\text{model}})$ extra parameters, no auxiliary
loss, and none of \textsc{concat}'s $C \mid d_{\text{model}}$
architectural constraint. \textsc{sum-ortho} is equivalent at
convergence but adds an auxiliary loss term that does not change the
outcome on our tasks; we suggest its main use is as a mild
training-speed accelerator when convergence budgets are tight.

\paragraph{What the FDM carrier buys (and doesn't).}
FDM is not what it sounds like. In communications, frequency-division
multiplexing prevents cross-talk on a \emph{shared} medium; in our
setting the blocks do not overlap, so there is no cross-talk to prevent
in the first place. What the sinusoidal carrier actually provides is an
intra-block RoPE-style positional prior: attention dot products between
two positions in the same channel gain a lag-dependent $\cos$ factor
without needing the model to learn the demix from the additive position
channel. Our experiments show this is a \emph{convergence-speed
advantage}: \textsc{fdm} reaches a given NLL sooner than \textsc{concat}
early in training (faster descent), but is eventually overtaken. At
convergence \textsc{concat} wins cleanly, and at larger
$d_{\text{model}}$ the gap widens because the fixed template cannot use
the extra width the way a learned projection can. A grid search over
11 fixed carrier bands and a learnable-$\omega_k$ variant both fail to
close this gap; the constraint is structural, not a hyperparameter
miss.

\paragraph{Architectural baselines.}
\textsc{ci} and \textsc{cat} give each channel nominally more capacity
than the block-partitioned encoders (full $d_{\text{model}}$ per channel
and per-$(t,k)$ token respectively) but both underperform at matched
parameter count. \textsc{ci} shares a single backbone across channels;
the bottleneck is the final-layer head's limited capacity to combine
$C$ per-channel streams. \textsc{cat} pays $\mathcal{O}((CT)^2)$
attention and gains nothing for it on this task, presumably because the
relevant structure is simultaneity of channels at a given $t$, not
long-range temporal dependencies across channels at different times.

\paragraph{What exactly fails about the \textsc{sum} baseline.}
Our \textsc{sum} baseline combines a shared scalar projection
$W \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$ with a per-channel additive
embedding $e_k$; this is the recipe most commonly seen in practitioner
codebases, and its total encoder capacity is $\mathcal{O}(d_{\text{model}})$
regardless of $C$. Under $15\times$ total-parameter scaling, its
validation NLL moves by less than $0.02$ nats. \textsc{sum-perch} (same
sum structure but with \emph{per-channel} learned projections $W_k$,
no orthogonality penalty) and \textsc{sum-ortho} (per-channel $W_k$
\emph{plus} the penalty) let us decompose the failure: the per-channel
$W_k$ upgrade contributes most of the improvement, and the orthogonality
penalty contributes the last bit. The bottleneck of \textsc{sum} is
therefore not summation per se---\textsc{sum-ortho} also sums---but the
combination of a shared scalar projection with a trivially small
per-channel identifier (the additive embedding $e_k$), which leaves no
gradient signal that would encourage allocation of orthogonal subspaces.

\paragraph{LayerNorm does not destroy channel separation.}
Standard pre-LayerNorm could in principle mix channel blocks (it
computes statistics across the full $d_{\text{model}}$ axis), but
empirically the linear probes at layer 3 recover each channel to
$R^2 \ge 0.92$. LayerNorm rescales each coordinate by a statistic
derived from all coordinates but does not linearly combine them; the
directional block structure survives.

\section{Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item The benchmark is synthetic and deliberately constructed to make
      channel identity informative. On tasks where channels are truly
      exchangeable or redundant, \textsc{sum} may not be penalised.
\item Scales tested: $d_{\text{model}} \in \{64, 128, 256\}$,
      $C \in \{4, 8, 16\}$, up to ${\sim}2.4\text{M}$ parameters. At
      larger model scales the interplay between encoders and overfitting
      may change.
\item The \textsc{cat} baseline is skipped at $C{=}16$ for compute
      reasons; comparing it at matched attention budget rather than
      matched parameters would be a fairer test.
\item Carrier hyperparameters for \textsc{fdm} were searched but we did
      not tune every design knob (carrier ladder base, per-block
      LayerNorm). A per-block group-norm variant may alter the
      \textsc{fdm} ceiling.
\item Five seeds per configuration; for the marginal differences inside
      the top tier, a larger seed budget would tighten confidence
      intervals.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Channel-as-token and channel-independent TSF.}
iTransformer \citep{liu2024itransformer} transposes the problem so each
variate is a token with full $d_{\text{model}}$ capacity; PatchTST
\citep{nie2023patchtst} runs an independent transformer per channel with
shared weights; Crossformer \citep{zhang2023crossformer} does both
directions. These approaches all preserve per-channel capacity at the
cost of longer sequences or per-channel compute. The block-partitioned
encoding studied here sits in a different corner of the design space:
$C$ channels share one $d_{\text{model}}$-wide token at each time, with
partitioning and identity preserved \emph{within} the embedding rather
than across tokens.

\paragraph{Rotary position embedding (RoPE).}
\citet{su2021roformer} encodes positional information with rotation by
frequency-graded angles. \textsc{fdm} re-uses the per-dimension
frequency ladder but applies it within each channel's block, with the
\emph{channel} carrier setting the outer scale.

\paragraph{Perceiver IO and Fourier-feature input encodings.}
\citet{jaegle2021perceiver} attaches explicit Fourier features per
modality to disambiguate inputs to a shared latent attention
bottleneck. \textsc{fdm} differs in that the modality identifier and
the signal value are \emph{multiplied} (amplitude modulation) rather
than concatenated, and dimensions are physically partitioned rather
than augmented.

\paragraph{Orthogonality regularisation.}
Soft-orthogonality penalties on weight matrices have been used for
conditioning and feature decorrelation
\citep{bansal2018gainsorthogonality}. \textsc{sum-ortho} applies the
same idea specifically to per-channel input projections; to our
knowledge this is a new use case in the multi-signal embedding
literature.

\paragraph{Frequency-domain input encoders.}
TimesNet \citep{wu2023timesnet} processes time-series in the frequency
domain with an explicit fast Fourier transform (FFT). Our work is dual:
we perform an inverse-FFT-like \emph{encoding} of a single value onto a
per-channel frequency, rather than transforming the signal itself.

\section{Conclusion}

On tasks where channel identity is informative, the common
shared-projection summation recipe has a capacity-independent ceiling.
The fix is to give each channel its own learned projection: three
different dressings of this idea---free-summed (\textsc{sum-perch}),
orthogonality-regularised (\textsc{sum-ortho}), or hard-partitioned
into blocks (\textsc{concat})---all converge to the same downstream
operating point, because the learned projections spontaneously
orient into near-orthogonal subspaces under the task loss. The
frequency-division analogy (\textsc{fdm}) provides a
faster-convergence-but-lower-ceiling variant: useful as an inductive
bias at small capacity, a constraint at larger ones. The architectural
alternatives from the time-series forecasting literature
(channel-as-token, channel-independent) underperform these three at
matched parameter budget.

The most practical recommendation is \textsc{sum-perch}: a one-line
change to existing sum-encoding pipelines, no architectural constraint,
no auxiliary loss. It matches everything fancier we measured.
