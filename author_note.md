# A Note from the (Human) Author

As is made clear in this repository and the manuscript, an AI model (Anthropic's Claude Opus via Claude Code) was extensively used in planning and running the experiments as well as writing the manuscript.
In fact, I personally typed practically no lines of code presented here, and the bulk of the text in the manuscript was generated by the AI model too.

One of the original drivers for this project was to try out how a cutting-edge AI performs in the role of a well-read and (sometimes overly) enthusiastic junior scientist. A fairly speculative idea (the FDM encoder described in the research log) was picked as the playground. This left me in the role of trying to come up with useful questions, 'senior direction', and sometimes the devil's advocate.
As is well known, these systems can produce amazingly accurate outputs at superhuman speeds. This enabled me to explore many more ideas and questions than I most likely would have had time for otherwise. The original idea was quickly abandoned, but the more profound question of spontaneous orthogonalisation emerged from the back-and-forths with the AI.

A recurring theme in the 'dialogs' emerged, however: like a proper junior scientist, the AI model was very keen to jump to conclusions. As a prominent example, the AI had trouble recognising the significance of the uncertainties in the results, and would constantly draw much stronger conclusions than the full data justified.

The initial version of the manuscript wouldn't have passed any critical inspection, but to be honest, neither would many first drafts of human-typed manuscripts.

To summarise, these kinds of AI tools can enable very fast iteration and testing of research hypotheses and generation of end-to-end code. However, based on this experience, the human in the loop needs to do quite a lot of critical thinking and ideation work to guide the research onto solid paths. At least for now.
